## 2. Candidate tasks

Each task is worth 2 points: 1 for the core answer and 1 for the required qualification or explanation. Award 0, 1, or 2; do not award partial credit based solely on confidence or fluent wording.

### T01 — Earth's orbital period and calendar
**Question:** Approximately how long does Earth take to complete one orbit around the Sun, and why is a leap day used in the calendar?

**Expected answer:** NASA gives approximately 365.25 days for Earth's orbital year. The Gregorian calendar uses a tropical-year basis of approximately 365.2422 days; leap days compensate for the fractional day. Under the Gregorian rule, years divisible by 4 are leap years except century years not divisible by 400. Avoid treating the orbital year and tropical year as exactly interchangeable.

**Scoring:** 1 point for approximately 365.25 days as the NASA page's rounded orbital figure; 1 point for explaining the fractional-day calendar correction. If the answer distinguishes the sidereal/orbital year from the tropical year, credit that distinction; do not penalize a concise answer that follows the question's approximate framing.

**Primary source:** NASA, Facts About Earth — https://science.nasa.gov/earth/facts/  
**Cross-checks:** NASA NSSDC Earth Fact Sheet — https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html ; U.S. Naval Observatory, Leap Years — https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/leap_years

### T02 — Earth's atmosphere composition
**Question:** What are the two dominant gases in Earth's dry atmosphere near the surface, and approximately what are their proportions?

**Expected answer:** Nitrogen about 78.08% and oxygen about 20.95%; other gases make up the remainder. Water vapor is variable and is not included in the dry-air percentages.

**Scoring:** 1 point for identifying nitrogen and oxygen; 1 point for approximate percentages and recognizing that remaining gases are present. Accept sensible rounding.

**Primary source:** NASA NSSDC Earth Fact Sheet — https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html  
**Cross-check:** NASA, Facts About Earth — https://science.nasa.gov/earth/facts/

### T03 — Does the Moon rotate?
**Question:** A person says, “The Moon does not rotate because we always see the same side.” Is that correct? Explain what is happening.

**Expected answer:** No. The Moon rotates once on its axis in approximately the same time it takes to orbit Earth (about 27.3 days), a state called synchronous rotation or tidal locking. This is why roughly the same hemisphere faces Earth. The roughly 29.5-day phase cycle is a different interval and should not be confused with the rotation/orbital period.

**Scoring:** 1 point for correcting the false premise and stating that the Moon rotates; 1 point for explaining synchronous rotation and distinguishing the approximately 27.3-day orbital/rotation period from the longer phase cycle. Accept appropriately qualified approximate values.

**Primary source:** NASA, Moon Facts — https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/  
**Cross-check:** NASA Glenn, Moon — https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/moon/

### T04 — Apollo lunar samples
**Question:** According to NASA, approximately how much lunar rock and soil did Apollo astronauts bring back to Earth in total?

**Expected answer:** Approximately 842 pounds, or 382 kilograms.

**Scoring:** 1 point for approximately 842 pounds or 382 kilograms; 1 point for making clear this is the total returned by Apollo missions, not the amount from one mission.

**Primary source:** NASA, Moon Facts — https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/

### T05 — SI mole
**Question:** How many specified elementary entities are in one mole under the SI definition?

**Expected answer:** Exactly 6.02214076 × 10^23 specified elementary entities. The entities must be specified (for example, atoms, molecules, or ions).

**Scoring:** 1 point for the exact numerical value; 1 point for noting that the type of entity must be specified. Accept equivalent scientific notation.

**Primary source:** NIST, Definitions of SI Base Units — https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/definitions-si-base-units

### T06 — SI meter
**Question:** What fixed physical constant is used in the modern SI definition of the meter, and what exact numerical value is assigned to it?

**Expected answer:** The speed of light in vacuum, assigned the exact value 299,792,458 meters per second (with the second defined through the cesium-133 frequency).

**Scoring:** 1 point for identifying the speed of light in vacuum; 1 point for the exact value and units. Do not accept the speed of light in a material as the defined constant.

**Primary source:** NIST, Definitions of SI Base Units — https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/definitions-si-base-units

### T07 — Weather and atmospheric layers
**Question:** In which of the five main atmospheric layers does almost all weather occur, and does that layer have the same height everywhere?

**Expected answer:** The troposphere. Its height varies with location and is generally higher near the equator than near the poles.

**Scoring:** 1 point for troposphere; 1 point for explaining that its height varies by latitude/location. Accept approximate values only if correctly qualified.

**Primary source:** NOAA, Layers of the Atmosphere — https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/atmosphere/layers-of-atmosphere

### T08 — What counts as precipitation?
**Question:** According to the USGS, what forms of water released from clouds count as precipitation? Is rain the only form?

**Expected answer:** Rain, freezing rain, sleet, snow, and hail. Rain is the most common form, but it is not the only form.

**Scoring:** 1 point for recognizing multiple forms; 1 point for identifying the listed forms or an equivalent complete list and stating that rain is not the only form.

**Primary source:** USGS, Precipitation and the Water Cycle — https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/precipitation-and-water-cycle

## 3. Candidate set balance and limitations

- Eight tasks span Earth science, astronomy, metrology, atmospheric science, and hydrology.
- Several tasks test simple retrieval; others test misconception correction, definitional precision, or distinctions between similar time periods.
- This is a small convenience set, not a representative sample of reasoning tasks.
- Many items have short factual answers. Results cannot establish broad reasoning ability or general intelligence.
- Some official sources are from the same institutions or related source families; source independence must not be overstated.
- The set does not yet test difficult multi-source synthesis, contested claims, or long-horizon research.
